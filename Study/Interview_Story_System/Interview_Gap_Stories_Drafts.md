## Story A: Cross-cultural / international  (fully drafted, verify light)
*Covers: cross-cultural collaboration, adoption, stakeholder empathy.*

**Full:** During the East West ERP rollout, two of our sites were in Mexico, and getting the Juarez and El Paso teams truly ready mattered as much as the technical migration.

I saw early that a one-size training approach would not land. There were language, process, and on-the-ground differences, and remote-only support would leave gaps.

So I went on-site in El Paso and Juarez, adapted the onboarding and training to how those teams actually worked, and made sure the compliance and financial steps were understood locally, not just documented centrally.

The Mexico sites went live ready, not just installed, and the cross-site rollout held together.

Meeting people where they are, across sites and cultures, is how I make adoption actually stick.

**Short:** On the East West rollout I supported the Mexico sites in person in El Paso and Juarez, adapting onboarding and training to how those teams worked, so they went live ready, not just installed.

## Story B: Prioritization / triage under competing deadlines  (fully drafted)
*Covers: prioritization, managing parallel work, delivering under a hard deadline.*

**Full:** The new warehouse and Amazon Robotics launch had a hard go-live date, and four systems workstreams, product families, GL accounts, BOMs, and site training, were all urgent at once.

I realized I could not treat them as equal. If the data foundation, the product families and BOMs, slipped, then training and go-live would collapse on top of it.

So I sequenced deliberately: I locked the data foundation first, ran the GL setup in parallel where it did not depend on that, and held training for the last safe window so people trained on the real, final configuration. I protected the critical path and said no to nice-to-have changes that put the date at risk.

Everything converged at go-live, systems and people ready at the same time, on schedule.

When everything is urgent, I protect the critical path and sequence the rest around it, rather than treating every task as equal.

**Short:** On the warehouse and Robotics launch, four workstreams were urgent at once. I locked the data foundation first, ran independent work in parallel, and held training for the final config, so we hit go-live with systems and people ready.

## Story C: Conflict / disagreement  (fully drafted from the account-recovery facts)
*Covers: conflict, difficult customer, influence, root-cause thinking.*

**Full:** One of the at-risk Aptean accounts was convinced the product was the problem and was ready to walk, and it was a real piece of the roughly one million dollars at risk.

When I dug in, the real issue was not the product. It was adoption gaps and stalled custom work. But telling a frustrated customer "it is not the product" the wrong way would have ended the relationship.

So I did not argue. I walked them through the specifics, where usage had dropped and which integration had stalled, and reframed it as a shared fix rather than a defense of the software. I took ownership, set a weekly cadence, and worked the actual gaps with them.

We turned that account from churning to retained, and it stayed.

When I disagree with a customer on the root cause, I lead with evidence and shared ownership, not with being right.

**Short:** An at-risk Aptean account blamed the product. I disagreed, but instead of arguing I walked them through the real adoption and integration gaps, reframed it as a shared fix, and kept the account.

## Story D: Failure / mistake and the lesson  (draft anchored to a real project, CONFIRM before using)
*Covers: ownership, learning from failure, resilience.*

**Pick the true one.** Choose the version that actually happened to you, then keep only real specifics:
- (a) A data-migration issue during the East West ETL, a set of records or a mapping that did not come across clean and had to be caught and reworked.
- (b) An early assumption in the payment integration about a party's readiness that proved wrong and cost time.
- (c) An account in the recovery effort that still churned despite the work, and what you changed after.

**Draft (version a, the most common and credible for an implementer, confirm it is true):** During the East West data migration, I hit a real setback: a portion of the data did not map cleanly into the new system, and I caught it later than I would have liked because I had assumed the source data was in better shape than it was.

I owned it right away. I paused the affected cutover step, rebuilt the mapping and validation, and re-ran it before anything reached production.

We still migrated clean, and I added a validation checkpoint to the process so that gap could not recur.

The lesson I carry: validate the data reality early, and never assume the source is as clean as it looks. It is why I build a checkpoint in before cutover now.

**Short:** During the East West migration I caught a data-mapping gap later than I should have; I owned it, rebuilt the validation, migrated clean, and added a checkpoint so it could not recur.

> Verify: only use this if a data or assumption issue like this genuinely happened. If your real setback was different, swap in the true event and keep the same shape (own it, fix it, what you changed).

## Story E: Acting on tough feedback  (draft anchored to a real instance, CONFIRM before using)
*Covers: self-awareness, coachability, growth. Doubles as a disarming answer to the "weakness" question.*

**Draft (confirm the real instance):** Earlier in my career, a manager told me directly that my updates ran too long and people were losing the point.

It stung, but it was true. I was giving the whole process when they only needed the headline and the one thing that mattered.

So I started leading with the outcome first, then offering detail only if asked, and I practiced it deliberately in status meetings and client calls.

My updates got sharper, meetings got shorter, and stakeholders started coming to me first for a clear read.

I take hard feedback as data and act on it fast, which is exactly why I have kept sharpening how I communicate.

**Short:** A manager once told me my updates were too long and buried the point. I took it seriously, started leading with the outcome and offering detail on request, and my communication got noticeably sharper.

> Verify: anchor this to a real person and moment. If your real tough feedback was about something else (pace, documentation, delegation), use that instead, same shape. Note: this is a strong, honest theme for you because it is true and it shows growth; just make it a real memory, not this exact wording.
