# Daily Review and Practice
### About 15 to 20 minutes a day. Builds calm, skill, and momentum in one sitting.

You are not broken. Interview nerves and a draining social world are trainable, and the fixes are skills, not willpower. This routine works several levers at once: the nervous-system tools that keep you steady, the communication and consultative delivery that make you persuasive without selling, and the small daily study reps that keep your momentum alive. It is built around how your mind actually works (top-down, meaning-driven, non-linear), and it is kept small enough that your anxiety never flags it as a threat. Full context lives in `IT_Learning_Path_and_Schedule.docx` and `How_I_Think_and_Communicate.md` in this folder.

## The three anchors (memorize all three)

- **Regulation:** "My brain is trying to keep me safe right now, but I am secure enough to handle this." Three slow belly breaths, feet on the floor, before anything else.
- **Consultative:** "I am not selling myself. I am diagnosing their problem."
- **Purpose:** "I help people adopt and understand AI safely." When motivation dips, it is a meaning problem, not a discipline problem. Reconnect to the why.

## Your positioning anchor (broad identity, sharp per role)

Your "one thing" is a capability, not a domain: you take ambiguous, cross-functional problems and turn them into structured delivery that people actually adopt. That shows up as program management, product ownership, continuous improvement, consulting, ERP implementation, or, increasingly, AI enablement and architecture. You specialize in the messy middle where business, operations, and technology have to line up, which is exactly where most initiatives break. Broad in where it applies; specialized in the capability.

Your 20-second umbrella pitch (over-rehearse until it is automatic and calm):
> "I take on ambiguous, cross-functional work, program, product, process, or system, and turn it into structured delivery that actually gets adopted. My best work is at the intersection of business, operations, and technology, where things usually break, and I have owned that from both the vendor and the customer side."

For AI-leaning roles, add the through-line: *"Lately that means helping teams adopt AI safely and effectively, pairing the systems view with hands-on tools."*

Then match ONE title to the role in front of you (Technical Program Manager, Product Owner, Solutions/Implementation Consultant, AI Enablement/Architect) and lead with the proof story that fits that lane.

Three reframes to say at full size, never apologetically:
- Not "I'm not tied to one tool/ERP" but "I ramp fast because I understand the logic and the business process; the interface changes, the fundamentals do not."
- Not "I'm not really a coder" but "configuration, integration coordination, SQL-level data work, and now building AI agents; I bridge business and dev."
- Not "almost desktop support" but "enterprise application support with SQL Server, Active Directory, and integration diagnostics."

Ban these self-sabotage words: "just", "almost", "not really", "I wouldn't advertise that." They take a strength and apologize for it.

## The daily routine

**Minutes 0 to 3, regulate and prime.** Three belly breaths, feet flat. Read your Purpose anchor. Then read a paragraph aloud slowly and read your top three wins from your Evidence Log out loud. This settles your nervous system and reminds it, before you practice anything, that you are competent.

**Minutes 3 to 6, study reps (keep momentum).** Do today's Anki cards (one deck), and one short foundations drill (subnetting or a small coding problem) if you are in that phase. Small, finishable, daily. Momentum beats intensity.

**Minutes 6 to 9, the speaking drill.** Run this week's focus: meat-first (answer in sentence one, bottom line up front), the hedge count (record and tally "I guess" / "kind of"), or the 30-60-120 (one story at three lengths). One drill, out loud, standing, on camera. Over-enunciate and slow down; this is your mumbling fix.

**Minutes 9 to 12, a consultative rep.** Pick one: rewrite one answer problem-first (client's bottleneck, then what you did, then the outcome), or draft three diagnostic questions you would ask a client or interviewer. This trains the doctor-patient stance: diagnose before you prescribe.

**Minutes 12 to 15, a mindset rep.** Pick one: write one anxious prediction and label it a theory, not a forecast; or name one thing you hesitate to claim and say why sharing it is a service, not bragging; or name one thing you are avoiding and shrink the next step until it is almost too small to refuse.

**Minutes 15 to 17, ground and score.** Say all three anchors once. Then a three-item scorecard: did I lead with the point, what was my hedge count, did I frame it as their problem first.

*On a low-energy day, drop to the anchors plus one Anki set plus one minute of reading aloud. That is a complete, successful day. Safety beats speed.*

## How you work best (so you stop fighting yourself)

- **Lead with the why and the point.** Your top-down mind is a communication strength; use it. Answer first, then support.
- **Attach a purpose to everything.** You learn and perform far better when a real goal is attached. Supply the meaning yourself.
- **Prepare and script.** Running on a rehearsed script is a strength, not cheating. It frees your attention for delivery.
- **Manage the senses.** Reduce glare and noise, keep water nearby, arrive early, and recover quietly after social or speaking exertion.
- **Be kind to the stumble.** A fumble is a rep, not a verdict. The harsh, all-or-nothing voice makes everything harder; treat mistakes as data.

## Interview-morning grounding sequence

Run this before a real call, not daily.

- **Lower the bar:** rehearse only your one 30-second anchor, out loud, standing. Not the whole guide.
- **Separate feelings from facts:** whatever your anxiety predicts, treat it as a theory to test, not a done deal.
- **Name the intent:** say the Regulation anchor. The nervousness is protection, not truth.
- **Expect the spike:** anxiety may climb right before you perform. That is the extinction burst. It means the change is working, not failing.
- **Prep the senses:** three breaths, water within reach, reduce glare and noise, join a minute early to settle.
- **End on proof:** read your Evidence Log once, especially the praise section, then breathe.

## Decision framework (for the hard-choice days)

- **The Why Filter:** does this serve the long-term purpose? If not, drop it, shrink it, or delegate it.
- **Reversible vs irreversible:** move fast on reversible decisions (most of them) and adjust later; slow down only on the few you cannot undo.
- **Time-box and go good-enough:** give the choice a deadline and a good-enough bar, decide, act, and refine. Waiting for certainty is its own cost.
- **Delegate and tool the low-level:** outsource fine-motor, linear, and admin work; let AI write the intermediate steps you naturally skip.

## Weekly focus (rotate, pairs with your speaking plan)

- **Week A:** meat-first and problem-first framing.
- **Week B:** kill the hedges and run the self-talk reframe.
- **Week C:** the story ladder and diagnostic questions.
- **Week D:** full mock on camera, then score against the three-item card.

## How to sell your projects (stop underselling)

Your default is to narrate what happened. Interviewers do not want a diary; they want to know the problem you owned, what you decided, and what changed because of you. Convert every project story into this shape:

1. **The problem, framed as stakes.** What was broken, who it hurt, and why it mattered to the business.
2. **Your specific ownership.** Not "we"; what *you* decided, coordinated, or built. Name the judgment calls.
3. **The outcome, with a number where you can.** Time saved, errors reduced, adoption gained, revenue or risk moved. If you lack a hard metric, use scope (systems, users, teams) or a before-and-after.
4. **The lesson or pattern.** One sentence on what it taught you, which signals depth and self-awareness.

Two rules: lead with the result or the stakes (top-down), not the chronology; and claim your contribution at full size. Sharing what you did well is a service to the interviewer trying to place you, not bragging. Keep five or six of these stories rehearsed, each proving a different strength, and reuse them across questions.
