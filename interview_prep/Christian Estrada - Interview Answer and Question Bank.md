# Interview Answer and Question Bank

For ERP, implementation, consulting, and project-manager interviews. Two parts: how to answer the questions you will actually get, and the questions to ask that make you look like an effective employee already in the room, not a candidate answering prompts.

Every answer uses the same shape: sentence one is the answer plus your ownership, then one closest example, then the outcome with a number, then stop. The model lines below are starting sentences, not scripts. Say them in your own words so they never sound canned.

---

## Part 0: Your positioning (lead with this; match the title to the role)

Your "one thing" is a capability, not a domain: you take ambiguous, cross-functional problems and turn
them into structured delivery that people actually adopt. That is what makes you both broad and
specialized. It shows up as program management, product ownership, continuous improvement, consulting,
or ERP implementation depending on the role. You are not a master of none. You are a master of the
messy middle where business, operations, and technology have to line up, which is exactly where most
initiatives break.

Umbrella pitch (default; adapt the title to whatever role you are in):
"I take on ambiguous, cross-functional work, program, product, process, or system, and turn it into
structured delivery that actually gets adopted. My best work is at the intersection of business,
operations, and technology, where things usually break, and I have owned that from both the vendor and
the customer side."

Pick ONE title per conversation, matched to the role in front of you (never a range):
- Program / delivery roles: "I'm targeting Technical Program Manager / Delivery roles."
- Product roles: "Product Owner / Product Manager."
- Process / operations: "Continuous Improvement / Business Process Analyst."
- Consulting / implementation: "Implementation or Solutions Consultant."

Then specialize the proof to that lane (lead with the story that fits):
- Program / delivery: owned the five-month EFT/ACH replacement and the warehouse + Amazon Robotics
  launch end to end, cross-functional, without direct authority.
- Product: de facto product owner of the ERP platform, requirements to backlog to adoption, drove
  VP and director decisions.
- Continuous improvement / process: cut manual work 78% and discrepancies 22% by redesigning a
  workflow; standardized processes across five sites.
- Consulting: discovery to delivery, translating ambiguous business needs into SOWs, scope, and
  adoption; McKinsey Forward.
- ERP / implementation: dual-sided Aptean Intuitive, vendor consultant and internal owner, full
  lifecycle.
- Analytics / operations: 200+ dashboards, SQL and Power BI, turning data into decisions.
- Customer success: protected $1M+ at-risk revenue across 80+ clients through QBRs and recovery.

Three reframes, said at full size (see "Recruiter call analysis and repositioning.md"):
- "You don't have our exact tool / ERP" -> "I ramp fast because I understand the underlying logic and
  the business process; the interface changes, the fundamentals do not."
- "Are you a developer?" -> "My technical work is configuration, integration coordination, and
  SQL-level data work. I'm not a developer, but I work closely with dev teams and read and write SQL."
- "Was that just support?" (Aderant) -> "Enterprise application support with SQL Server, Active
  Directory, and integration diagnostics, my most technical role."

Never say: "just", "almost", "not really", "I wouldn't advertise that". Attach a number to every role.

## Part A: Common questions and how to answer

### ERP and implementation

1. Walk me through an implementation you owned.
Lead: "I led international manufacturing clients from ambiguous requirements to a working go-live in Aptean Encompix." Then name the arc in one breath: discovery, requirements, configuration, data migration, testing, training, go-live, and post-go-live support. Close: "Because I stayed through go-live, adoption actually held." Move: sound like you own the whole lifecycle, not a task in it.

2. How do you handle scope creep or changing requirements?
Lead: "I control scope at the source, in discovery and the SOW, not after the build starts." Anchor: hundreds of SOW and FRD documents where you set validated scope, milestones, and cost baselines up front. Move: show you prevent it, not just absorb it.

3. Tell me about a difficult stakeholder or competing priorities.
Lead: "I make sure each group is heard before requirements are finalized, then I turn their tradeoffs into a decision." Anchor: five sites and 150-plus users, turning operations, finance, and engineering needs into scoped recommendations for VPs and directors. Move: alignment without authority is the skill; name it.

4. How do you approach data migration or go-live risk?
Lead: "I treat data and integration as a validation problem, not just a technical task." Anchor: ETL and SQL checks, cutover coordination, least-privilege access, and a signoff path; the five-month EFT/ACH replacement with a compliance angle. Move: source clarity, field-level checks, test cases, exception handling, signoff before go-live.

5. Tell me about a project that went wrong.
Lead honestly: "I inherited a broken implementation, resolved every technical issue, and we still lost the account." Then the lesson: trust is not only technical; I now name relationship risk early, not just defects. Move: self-aware and low-risk, which is what this question is really testing.

### Consulting and solutions

6. How do you handle ambiguity?
Lead: "I run a structured discovery-to-go-live approach and adapt it, rather than forcing a rigid framework." Anchor: turning vague asks into SOWs, functional requirements, and test plans. Move: your method is a deliberate choice, never an apology.

7. How do you explain a technical product to a non-technical audience?
Lead: "I start with who uses it, the business problem it solves, and the value in plain language." Anchor: pre-sales to make-to-order and food-and-beverage manufacturers, and 60-plus executive workshops. Move: translation is the consulting skill; show it.

8. How do you build credibility fast in a new domain?
Lead: "I map the new domain to a framework I already know, then validate it with stakeholders." Anchor: reading a new ERP-like product against your ERP background, and learning an unfamiliar industry's language. Move: fast comprehension plus humility, not fake expertise.

### Project and program management

9. Tell me about the largest project you managed.
Lead with one story, told lean: the five-month EFT/ACH replacement across internal IT, global finance, Aptean, and Truist Bank, or the warehouse and Amazon Robotics launch. Sentence one, one example, the outcome, stop.

10. What methodology do you use?
Lead: "Structured discovery-to-go-live, adapted to the project." Discovery, scope, stakeholder alignment, milestone plan with checkpoints, build and validate, go-live, adoption. Move: do not say "we did not really follow a framework"; state yours as intentional.

11. How do you run a project when you have no formal authority?
Lead: "I get the people who own the outcome bought into a milestone plan they can commit to." Anchor: four parties on EFT/ACH, five sites on the warehouse launch, all without direct reports. Move: influence and ownership, not title.

12. How do you handle risk or a slipping timeline?
Lead: "I make risk visible early, with an owner and a next step for each one." Anchor: weekly recovery sessions and visible risks and owners during migration and go-live. Move: proactive control, not heroics at the end.

### Behavioral and screening

13. Tell me about yourself.
Lead: "I am an enterprise systems and delivery person, and the consistent thread is turning ambiguous problems into systems and adoption that hold." One proof (200-plus dashboards, or 80-plus clients across a 6 million dollar book). Then stop. Do not narrate your whole career.

14. Why did your most recent role end?
Lead: "It ended due to a company reorganization." One line of scope: you were the primary owner of the ERP environment across five sites. Stop. This is your single biggest rambling trap; keep it three sentences.

15. What is a weakness or a gap we should know about?
Lead: name one honest adjacent-not-deep area, then how you close it. Anchor: "My depth is implementation judgment, data validation, training, and issue ownership; when a domain is new, I ramp by mapping it to what I know and validating fast." Move: honest and low-risk.

16. Why this company or role?
Lead with your company hypothesis, then fit: "It looks like your challenge is scaling delivery faster than the process can keep up, and that is exactly the ambiguity-to-structure work I do." Move: do the research; specificity beats enthusiasm.

## Part B: Questions to ask (be the effective employee, not the interviewee)

The move that separates you: do not save all questions for the end. Ask them during the conversation, build on what they say ("You mentioned X, how does that play out when..."), and reflect their problem back. That turns the call into a working meeting. Pick three, and always lead with outcome and problem questions.

Outcome and success (ask these first):
- "What does success look like in the first three to six months for this role?"
- "How will you know, a year from now, that this was a great hire?"

The real problem (this is where you sound like a consultant):
- "What is the biggest problem you are hoping this person solves in the first year?"
- "Where does this function or team most often get stuck today?"
- "What is currently working that you would not want a new person to break?"

Stakeholders and how work happens:
- "Who are the key stakeholders this role depends on, and what does each of them care about most?"
- "When priorities compete here, how do decisions actually get made?"

Team and bar:
- "What separates someone who is good in this role from someone who is great?"
- "Where is the team strongest, and where is it stretched thin right now?"

Strategic, for senior or final rounds:
- "What is changing in your market that this role needs to get ahead of?"
- "What would you want this person to challenge or improve, not just maintain?"

Two to avoid early: tooling and process minutiae ("which ticketing system do you use"), and anything answered on the careers page. Those read as tactical and have cost you rounds; save them for later or for a follow-up email.

Close-the-loop line for the end: "Based on our conversation, I am confident I can [their stated top priority]. What are the next steps, and is there anything about my background you would want me to address before then?"
