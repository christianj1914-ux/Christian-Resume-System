# Project Delivery Interview Stories

Two project-management stories written to match your interview system's framework, not generic STAR.

How your framework works, so you can relax about the label:
- Default shape is Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration. Hook names the business or delivery problem, Noticing shows the judgment you had before acting, Action and Result prove the work with scope and numbers, and Bridge and Calibration tie it to the role and check the interviewer's priority.
- For consulting, advisory, senior, or strategic interviews (Solutions Consultant, Randstad), it switches to CART: Context (impact first), Approach (how you structured the thinking), Role (your personal contribution without overstating authority), Transformation (what changed and why it matters here).
- Three modes: full 60 to 90 seconds, medium (Hook plus Action plus Result), and short (Result plus one line).
- Use PREP (Point, Reason, Example, Point) for yes/no or under-20-second questions, and philosophy-first when asked "how do you handle X."
- The beats are a skeleton. Do not recite them out loud.

On your worry about not following a formal methodology: you do have a method, you just do not label it. It is structured discovery-to-go-live, discovery, then scope and requirements, then stakeholder alignment, then a milestone plan with checkpoints, then build and validate, then cutover and go-live, then adoption. Say that plainly and it reads as judgment, not a gap.

## Story 1: EFT/ACH payment integration replacement

Anchor facts: five-month cross-functional initiative; replaced an outdated EFT/ACH file integration; coordinated internal IT, global finance, Aptean, and Truist Bank; deployed a compliant, auditable payment workflow; closed a long-standing infrastructure gap and restored payment process integrity; you owned scope, milestones, and coordination without direct authority.

### Full mode (Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration)

At East West, our EFT and ACH payments still ran through a years-outdated file integration, and it had become a real risk to paying vendors and running finance reliably.

Early on I noticed the problem was not really technical. It spanned our internal IT, our global finance team, our ERP vendor Aptean, and Truist Bank, and no one owned the whole chain, so failures kept falling between the seams.

I took ownership of the end-to-end effort over about five months. I mapped the current flow, defined the scope and the compliance requirements up front, set milestones each group could actually commit to, and ran the coordination across all four parties while we designed, validated, and deployed a new payment workflow.

We closed a long-standing infrastructure gap and restored payment process integrity, replacing a fragile setup with one that was compliant and auditable.

That is the kind of cross-functional delivery I would bring here: taking an ambiguous, multi-party problem and giving it just enough structure that the work actually moves.

Is that the kind of delivery challenge this role runs into most, or is it something different?

### CART mode (for consulting or senior rounds)

Context: I closed a long-standing payment-infrastructure gap at East West by replacing a failing EFT/ACH integration over about five months.

Approach: I started by mapping where payments were breaking down and saw the real issue was ownership across four parties, IT, global finance, Aptean, and Truist Bank, so I set the compliance requirements and a milestone plan each group could commit to before any build.

Role: I owned the coordination and the delivery end to end, without formal authority over those teams, and kept everyone aligned to the plan.

Transformation: the result was a compliant, auditable payment workflow that restored process integrity. The lesson I carry is that most delivery risk is really ownership and alignment risk, which is exactly what I would focus on in this role.

### Short mode
I owned a five-month effort at East West that replaced a failing EFT/ACH integration and restored reliable, compliant payments, coordinating IT, global finance, our ERP vendor, and the bank end to end.

## Story 2: New warehouse and Amazon Robotics systems launch

Anchor facts: launched a production-ready system setup for a new warehouse operation and an Amazon Robotics program; scoped product families, GL accounts, and BOMs; ran cross-site training; drove it from initial requirements through go-live across concurrent workstreams in a five-site, 150-plus-user environment.

### Full mode (Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration)

East West stood up a new warehouse operation with an Amazon Robotics program, and the systems side had to be production-ready by go-live or the whole launch would stall.

I saw early that this was not one project but several concurrent workstreams, product families, GL accounts, BOMs, and site training, that all had to land together, so sequencing and cross-site coordination mattered more than any single task.

I owned the system setup from initial requirements through go-live. I scoped the product families, GL accounts, and BOMs, built the milestone plan across the workstreams, and ran the cross-site training so users were ready when we cut over.

We hit a production-ready go-live for the warehouse and the robotics program, with the systems and the people ready at the same time.

That end-to-end ownership across parallel workstreams is what I would bring to a delivery role like this one.

Does your delivery work tend to run as parallel workstreams like that, or more sequential?

### CART mode (for consulting or senior rounds)

Context: I delivered a production-ready systems launch for a new warehouse and an Amazon Robotics program at East West, ready on time for go-live.

Approach: I treated it as several concurrent workstreams rather than one project, product families, GL accounts, BOMs, and site training, and sequenced them so they would converge at go-live.

Role: I owned the system setup end to end and ran the cross-site training myself so users were ready at cutover.

Transformation: both the systems and the people were ready at the same time. That habit of getting adoption ready alongside the build is what I would bring to your delivery programs.

### Short mode
I owned the systems launch for a new warehouse and an Amazon Robotics program at East West, scoping product families, GL accounts, and BOMs and running cross-site training to a production-ready go-live.

## Bonus: "How do you run a project?" (philosophy-first)

Lead with the method, then one story as evidence:

"I run a structured discovery-to-go-live approach. I start with discovery to understand the real problem, then define scope and requirements, align the stakeholders who own the outcome, and set a milestone plan with checkpoints. Then we build and validate, cut over to go-live, and I stay through adoption so it actually holds. I adapt that to the project rather than forcing a rigid framework. For example, on a five-month payment-integration replacement at East West, that structure is what kept four different parties moving together." Then stop and let them ask for the detail.

## Story 3: High-volume inventory automation (78/22)

Anchor facts: at East West, high-volume inventory adjustments were manual and error-prone; you built an automated, auditable workflow and did the same for Approved Manufacturer List maintenance; cut manual work by 78 percent and inventory discrepancies by 22 percent, and added an audit trail.

### Full mode (Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration)

At East West, our high-volume inventory adjustments were done manually, one at a time, which ate hours and quietly put errors into numbers people trusted.

I noticed the real cost was not just the time. Manual entry meant no audit trail, so discrepancies were hard to catch and harder to explain.

I built an automated, auditable workflow for the bulk adjustments, and did the same for Approved Manufacturer List maintenance, so work that used to be manual entry became a controlled, repeatable process.

That cut the manual work by 78 percent and reduced inventory discrepancies by 22 percent, and it gave us a clean audit trail we did not have before.

That is my instinct in an operations or systems role: find the manual, error-prone process and turn it into something automated and trustworthy.

Is process improvement like that a big part of this role, or is it more reporting and analysis?

### CART mode
Context: I cut manual work on high-volume inventory adjustments by 78 percent and discrepancies by 22 percent at East West by automating a process that used to be all manual entry. Approach: I saw the real problem was not speed but the missing audit trail, so I designed an automated, auditable workflow and extended it to Approved Manufacturer List maintenance. Role: I built the workflow myself and owned the rollout to the users who relied on it. Transformation: less manual work, fewer errors, and a clean audit trail, and the lesson that trustworthy process beats faster manual effort is what I would bring to your operations.

### Short mode
I automated high-volume inventory adjustments at East West, cutting manual work 78 percent and discrepancies 22 percent while adding an audit trail we did not have.

## Story 4: At-risk account recovery ($1M)

Anchor facts: at Aptean, inside a $6M-plus book of business, several key accounts were heading for churn with roughly $1M in annual revenue at risk; you diagnosed adoption, integration, and customization risks, consolidated fragmented case ownership, ran weekly recovery sessions, and converted at-risk relationships into retained accounts.

### Full mode (Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration)

Inside a 6 million dollar book of business at Aptean, several key accounts were sliding toward churn, and about 1 million dollars of annual revenue was genuinely at risk.

When I dug in, the churn signals were not really about the product. They were adoption gaps, integration issues, and custom work that had stalled, and case ownership was scattered, so nothing moved.

I consolidated case ownership so each at-risk account had one clear owner, ran weekly recovery sessions to drive the fixes, and worked the adoption and integration problems directly with the customers.

We protected the 1 million dollars in at-risk annual revenue and converted those shaky relationships back into retained, stable accounts.

That is what I do well in a customer-facing role: name the real risk early, take ownership, and turn a relationship around before it is lost.

Is at-risk recovery like that part of this role, or is it more about growing already-healthy accounts?

### CART mode
Context: I protected over 1 million dollars in at-risk annual revenue inside a 6 million dollar book at Aptean by recovering several accounts heading for churn. Approach: I diagnosed the churn as adoption, integration, and customization problems rather than product problems, and saw that fragmented case ownership was letting them drift. Role: I consolidated case ownership, ran the weekly recovery cadence, and worked the fixes with customers directly. Transformation: the at-risk revenue was retained and the relationships stabilized, and the lesson that most churn is an ownership and follow-through problem is what I would bring to your accounts.

### Short mode
I protected over 1 million dollars in at-risk revenue in a 6 million dollar Aptean book by consolidating case ownership and running a weekly recovery cadence that turned churning accounts around.

## Story 5: Zero-to-one SMS support channel

Anchor facts: at The Home Depot, as a founding pilot-team member, you helped stand up an internal SMS support channel that did not previously exist; you configured LivePerson LiveEngage chat and text workflows, automated greetings and closings, AI-assisted chatbot logic, and early channel-usage monitoring, and documented the setup so it could be repeated.

### Full mode (Hook, Noticing, Action, Result, Bridge, Calibration)

At The Home Depot, customers had no way to reach support over text, and we set out to stand up an SMS support channel that simply did not exist yet.

Because it was zero to one, there was no template to copy. I realized the hard part was designing the workflow itself, how a text conversation should open, route, and close, before any of it could scale.

As a founding member of the pilot team, I configured the LivePerson LiveEngage chat and text workflows, built the automated greetings and closings and the AI-assisted chatbot logic, and set up early channel-usage monitoring so we could see what was actually happening.

We launched a working SMS support channel that had not existed before, and I documented the setup so it could be repeated consistently.

Building something from nothing, and making it repeatable, is what I would bring to a role that needs new workflows stood up, not just existing ones maintained.

Does this role involve standing up new processes, or mostly running established ones?

### CART mode
Context: I helped launch a brand-new internal SMS support channel at The Home Depot, a customer communication workflow that did not previously exist. Approach: with no template to copy, I focused first on designing the conversation workflow itself, how texts open, route, and close, then built the automation and monitoring around it. Role: as a founding pilot-team member I configured the LiveEngage chat and text workflows, the automated greetings and closings, the AI-assisted chatbot logic, and the early usage monitoring. Transformation: we stood up a working channel from zero and made the setup repeatable, and the lesson that a new workflow lives or dies on its design before it scales is what I would bring here.

### Short mode
I helped stand up a zero-to-one SMS support channel at The Home Depot, configuring the LiveEngage chat and text workflows, chatbot logic, and monitoring for a channel that did not exist before.
