# Interview Repair Kit and Dematic Mock

## The honest assessment (read this once, then move to the drills)

You are a capable candidate with a delivery problem, not a capability problem. That is the
whole diagnosis. Across Dematic and State Farm the pattern is identical: the substance was
there, but you improvised the structure live, so you rambled, buried your best evidence, and
made the interviewer do the work of finding your value. Interviewers do not score raw
experience; they score how clearly you demonstrate the competencies on their hidden scorecard.
When you improvise, you leave that scoring to chance.

Three shortcomings, stated plainly:

1. You answer like you are thinking out loud instead of leading. You warm up, wander through
   context, then arrive at the point. By then the interviewer has already formed an impression
   of "unclear."
2. You do not pre-map the role's scorecard. You walk in without knowing the four to eight
   competencies and the specific frameworks and keywords they will listen for, so you cannot
   aim your stories. The State Farm Lean Six Sigma miss is the clearest example: you had the
   experience, you just had not pre-loaded a story and a framework name.
3. You treat gaps passively. You admit the gap honestly (good) but stop there instead of
   pivoting into proof of how fast you close gaps (bad). Honesty without a bridge reads as a
   weakness; honesty with a bridge reads as self-awareness.

None of this means you are not good enough. It means you have not been rehearsing the right
things. That is fixable in weeks, not years.

---

## The one structure that fixes 80% of it: BLUF

Every answer, every time: **Answer -> Example -> Result -> Relevance.** Bottom Line Up Front.

- Answer: the direct point in one sentence.
- Example: one specific story, not three half-stories.
- Result: the outcome, with a number if you have one.
- Relevance: one sentence connecting it to THIS role.

Then stop. Silence after a tight answer reads as confidence. Trailing off reads as
uncertainty.

Verbal signposts make you sound senior instantly: "There were three parts to that."
"The core problem was X." "What I changed was Y." "Why that matters here is Z." Signposting
forces structure on you in real time even when you are nervous.

## Your five anchor stories (memorize these, tell each in 60-90 seconds)

These are your real stories, restructured into BLUF/STAR. Rehearse them out loud until they
are automatic. Fill any bracketed blanks with your real specifics.

**Story 1 - Discovery that uncovered a hidden risk (Aptean, Windows 95 migration)**
- Situation: A manufacturing client was running production on Windows-95-era systems and wanted
  to modernize.
- Task: Lead pre-sales discovery to scope what a migration would actually take.
- Action: I mapped their current ERP/WMS, inventoried hardware and server compatibility, and
  surfaced the real risk everyone else missed: the third-party integrations. Custom reporting,
  ODBC connections, and interface troubleshooting were where the project would live or die, so
  I documented those up front instead of discovering them mid-build.
- Result: We migrated their data to modern infrastructure and the integrations held, because we
  scoped them in discovery instead of hitting them in production.
- Relevance: That is exactly this role, taking everything out of the customer's head, getting
  it on paper, and catching the integration risks before they become change orders.

**Story 2 - Customer conflict and expectation reset (Aptean, CEO escalation)**
- Situation: A client's CEO and board felt the delivered product looked nothing like what had
  been agreed years earlier.
- Action: I took direct ownership. I set weekly meetings with the CEO, coordinated internally
  with product and dev to fast-track fixes already in the pipeline, and validated every fix in
  a test database before it touched their environment. Then I changed how I closed the loop: I
  write an FRD after every live meeting and send it back asking "is this exactly what we
  agreed, or is there more you need," and I widen the email chain so alignment is documented on
  both sides.
- Result: We rebuilt the relationship and kept the account. More importantly it became my
  standard practice, because people agree in the room and react differently when they see it in
  writing.
- Relevance: Discovery work lives or dies on documented alignment; this is how I prevent the
  "this isn't what we asked for" conversation from ever happening.

**Story 3 - Fast ramp on an unfamiliar system (East West, thrown into the fire)**
- Situation: East West recruited me to own an Aptean configuration I had never used.
- Action: I ramped fast by mapping the new system to the ERP logic I already knew, validated my
  understanding against the actual workflows, and within [weeks] I was training global teams on
  it.
- Result: I went from unfamiliar to the person others learned it from, [timeframe].
- Relevance: I do not need to already know your conveyors or your specific platform. My track
  record is ramping quickly on unfamiliar systems and then teaching them, and that is the exact
  pattern I would bring to your hardware side.

**Story 4 - Cross-functional alignment without authority (East West, EFT/ACH replacement)**
- Situation: A years-outdated EFT/ACH payment integration had to be replaced, with ownership
  split across internal IT, global finance, the vendor, and Truist Bank.
- Action: The real problem was not technical, it was that no one owned the whole path. I created
  one accountable delivery path across all four parties, ran it on a five-month timeline with
  visible milestones and risks, and validated the workflow was compliant and auditable before
  cutover.
- Result: We replaced a fragile setup with a compliant, auditable payment workflow and closed a
  long-standing gap.
- Relevance: Solutions work is aligning business, IT, and vendors who do not naturally talk to
  each other, and turning that into one plan people commit to.

**Story 5 - Process improvement / troubleshooting a messy problem (East West, inventory
automation) - this is also your Lean Six Sigma story**
- Situation: Manual inventory processing was slow and error-prone, with high discrepancy and
  scrap.
- Action: I ran it like a root-cause problem. I defined the failure, measured the baseline,
  traced the errors to their source (people were mis-processing work orders and causing losses),
  and redesigned the process into an automated, auditable correction workflow, then locked it in
  with least-privilege controls so the errors could not recur.
- Result: 78% less manual processing, 22% fewer discrepancies, and about 32% lower scrap cost.
- Relevance: I have not sat for a Six Sigma belt, but this is DMAIC in practice, define,
  measure, analyze, improve, control, and I work comfortably embedded with Lean Six Sigma teams.

**Q2 - Walk me through what you did at East West.** (Lead with the software category unprompted.)
"My work there was on the business-systems and analytics side, not PLC programming or writing

the system accurate, Power BI and SQL reporting so leadership could actually see operations,
and access governance. The access piece has a story: employees were mis-processing work orders

(Notice: categorizes the software in sentence one, three signposted parts, leads with the strong
least-privilege story instead of burying it.)

**Q3 - Tell me about Aptean.** (Use Story 1, tightened.)

## The method that would have saved the State Farm interview: the JD scorecard

Before any interview, build a one-page scorecard from the job description:

1. List the competencies they will score (usually 4-8): pull them from the JD's "what you'll
   do" and "what you need" sections. For a Solutions Consultant: discovery, requirements
   translation, stakeholder alignment, structured problem solving, customer communication,
   adaptability.
2. For each competency, write the frameworks and keywords to name out loud. This is the part you
   are missing. Examples:
   - Process improvement -> DMAIC, PDCA, 5 Whys, root-cause analysis, value stream mapping,
     Lean Six Sigma.
   - Project delivery -> Agile, Scrum, sprint, backlog, milestone, risk register, change control.
   - Discovery -> current-state mapping, requirements elicitation, FRD/SOW, gap analysis.
   - Data -> KPI, dashboard, data quality, ETL validation.
3. Map one anchor story to each competency (use the five above; most cover several).
4. Write your gap pivots for anything you are thin on (hardware, formal Six Sigma belt, a
   specific tool): admit, bridge to a ramp story, ask a smart question.

On the Lean Six Sigma question specifically, the prepared answer is: "I have not sat for a
formal belt, but I have worked embedded with Lean Six Sigma teams and I run improvement work the
DMAIC way. For example [Story 5]: I defined the scrap and discrepancy problem, measured the
baseline, traced root cause, redesigned and automated the process, and locked in controls,
78% less manual work and about 32% lower scrap. The framework I reach for most is root-cause
plus 5 Whys before I touch the process." That is a strong answer to a "gap," because you named
the framework, gave a quantified story, and showed a point of view.

## Skills genuinely worth building (honest, not padding)

I do not have your IT skills learning-path thread, so tell me what is in it and I will align to
it. Based on what I can see, in priority order:

1. Structured executive communication. This is your highest-ROI skill and it is the actual thing

2. Formal methodology fluency, enough to name and use, not to certify: Lean Six Sigma / DMAIC,

   where your discovery instinct already shines.
4. PMP (you are pursuing it) and a cloud fundamentals cert (AWS or Azure) are credible signals
   for the solutions/PM lane.

Be careful with one thing: you do not need to become a developer or a PLC programmer to win
Solutions Consultant roles. Your edge is discovery, translation, and implementation judgment.
Sharpen the edge you have; do not chase a different job's skills.

## Daily prep additions (15 minutes, folds into your existing routine)

  Answer-Example-Result-Relevance, under 90 seconds, on camera. Watch for warm-up phrases.
- One story rep: run one of the five anchor stories; score yourself on whether you led with the
  point and named a number.
- One scorecard rep: take any live JD, list its 4-8 competencies and the frameworks/keywords for
  each. Speed matters; you want this to take five minutes before a real interview.
- One gap-pivot rep: name something you are thin on and practice admit-bridge-ask.
- Kill-list: tally "broadly speaking," "in my background," "there were a few things," "it kind
  of depended." Target zero.
